---

I've come to a simple rule for client walkthroughs, and this piece explains it: why the person who built it should drive the demo, and the project manager should run the room. It sounds like a small matter of who sits where. In practice it decides whether the client sees the real thing or a retelling of it, and whether your best people are still willing to show up next time.

## The client's clock is the clock

Clients tend to expect the vendor to work on the client's hours. That's normal, and I don't argue with it. It does mean the vendor team has to manage its own time, because nobody else will.

In practice this shows up in where sessions land. Client meetings tend to fall in the client's afternoon. For part of my team, that afternoon is the middle of the night. A two-hour workshop the client books for mid-afternoon their time becomes a two-hour session at dawn for an engineer on the other side of the world.

I learned how easy that is to miss. A draft message once very nearly put an engineer into a dawn slot without anyone noticing. Someone stopped and did the time-zone arithmetic before it went out, and that's the only reason it got caught. Nothing in the process would have caught it. One person happened to check the maths.

So the first problem is scheduling, and it comes before any question of who drives the demo.

## Say in the plan which sessions need a builder

Most client meetings don't need the people who built the product. Status updates, scope conversations and routine check-ins can run without them. Some sessions do need them: a walkthrough, a workshop, anything where the client will look at the actual screens and ask how things work.

My rule now is to write it into the plan. Next to each important session, the plan says whether a builder has to be there. Once that's written down, the scheduling question changes. You stop asking "when is the room free?" and start asking "when can this person realistically be there?"

That difference matters. If you schedule around the room, the builder gets whatever slot is left, and the slot left is often the one at dawn. If you schedule around the person, you see the cost before you commit to it. Sometimes you still choose the dawn slot. But you choose it on purpose, with the person's agreement, and not by accident in a calendar invite.

## The builder drives the screens

When a builder is in the session, they drive. They share the screen, click through the flows, and answer the questions about how it works.

I don't relay demonstrations any more. When someone who didn't build the thing walks the client through it, the client is watching a paraphrase. It's the same failure as paraphrasing a quote. Each step loses a little of the meaning: the reason a screen works the way it does, the edge case the builder already handled, the honest answer to "what happens if…". The person relaying can't supply those, because they weren't there when the decisions were made.

The builder can. When a client asks something unexpected, the builder answers from the work itself and doesn't have to guess what the work does. I don't have a better reason than that for putting the builder in the driving seat. The client gets the product from the person who made it.

## The project manager runs the room

Driving the screens is one job. Running the room is another, and the builder shouldn't have to do both.

The project manager owns the session. They open it, keep it to time, track the questions, steer the discussion back when it drifts, note what was agreed and handle follow-ups. The builder can then concentrate on showing the work and answering about it, without also watching the clock and the agenda.

When I set up a walkthrough, I give the project manager the instruction in a single line: the builder drives the screens, you run the room. It's short on purpose. It tells each person what they own and what they don't. Nobody stands in for anyone else, and nobody covers both jobs.

## Ask once, get a yes, and stop asking

The third part is the one I'd most like other managers to take on board.

When a session needs a builder at an awkward hour, I ask them directly. I don't send the request through the project manager, and I don't take it for granted. I ask whether they'll drive the screens for that session. If they say yes, it's settled.

Then I make sure nobody asks again. Not the project manager, not a follow-up message, not a "just confirming" note the day before. I tell the project manager plainly not to ask a second time.

The reason is simple. A dawn slot agreed to once is goodwill. The same request made twice is a cost. The second ask suggests you didn't trust the first answer. It makes the person spend a moment deciding all over again whether to give up their early morning. It turns a favour into an obligation that keeps getting re-checked. Goodwill is limited, and repeating the request is a reliable way to use it up.

## Putting it together

My approach comes down to three habits:

1. **Say in the plan which sessions need a builder present**, and schedule those around the person, not around the room.
2. **The person who built it drives the screens. The project manager runs the room.** Don't relay a demonstration.
3. **Ask once, get a yes, and stop asking.**

None of this is complicated. Each habit fixes a failure that's easy to fall into without noticing: a calendar invite that quietly costs someone their sleep, a walkthrough that turns into a secondhand summary, a request repeated until it starts to grate.

The client works to their own clock, and I accept that. Within it, I can still decide who does which job and how often I ask people for their time. The client sees the work from the person who built it, and the session runs well because someone else is running it.
