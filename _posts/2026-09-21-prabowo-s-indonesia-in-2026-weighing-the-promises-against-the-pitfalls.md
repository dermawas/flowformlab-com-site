**TL;DR:** Two years into office, Prabowo's push for a strong, active state has delivered political stability, direct welfare programs like free school meals, a balanced foreign policy and progress in mineral processing, but it has also brought fiscal strain, messy program delivery, governance risks around Danantara and worries about democratic backsliding. The article argues that the strengths and weaknesses come from the same source, and that whether the promises beat the pitfalls depends less on the vision than on budget discipline, accountability and the everyday work of governing well.

---

Indonesia's presidency in 2026 is at an awkward middle stage. The opening months are over, and the next election is still years away. Prabowo Subianto will mark two years in office this October, which is long enough for his biggest ideas to become real programs with real budgets. It's also long enough for the costs to show. Looking at the pros and cons isn't about cheering or complaining. It's about asking whether the country's direction matches the scale of what it hopes to become.

## The Big Bet: A State That Does More

Prabowo came to power with a simple message: the state should do more, do it faster and reach people directly. That thinking shows up across his agenda.

The flagship is the **Free Nutritious Meals program (Makan Bergizi Gratis)**. It aims to feed tens of millions of schoolchildren, pregnant women and toddlers. Other programs sit alongside it:

- **Danantara**, a sovereign wealth fund that pulls state-owned enterprise assets under one roof to fund strategic investment
- **Village cooperatives (Koperasi Desa Merah Putih)**, meant to strengthen rural economies and cut out middlemen
- **Free health screenings** and **Sekolah Rakyat** boarding schools for children from poor families
- A continued push for **food and energy self-sufficiency** and **downstreaming**, meaning processing nickel and other minerals at home instead of exporting raw ore

All of this rests on one ambition: annual economic growth of 8%, well above the roughly 5% Indonesia has averaged for years.

## The Case For: Where the Presidency Is Delivering

### Political stability and a clear direction

Prabowo governs with a very large coalition. That makes getting things done much easier. Laws pass, budgets get approved and ministries know what the priorities are. For a country that often struggles to turn plans into action, that clarity is worth something.

### Welfare that reaches people directly

Whatever its flaws, the meals program goes straight at a real problem: stunting and poor nutrition in children. Stunting rates have fallen over the past decade but are still high. Feeding children every day is concrete and visible, and it can change lives if it's done well. The program also creates demand for local farmers, fishers and small kitchens.

### A confident, balanced foreign policy

Indonesia joined BRICS in early 2025. It has also kept up its ties with Western partners, finished the long-delayed trade agreement with the European Union and negotiated with Washington through the tariff turbulence. The result is a foreign policy that keeps options open and uses Indonesia's size as leverage. For a middle power, that's a sensible position.

### Moving up the value chain

Downstreaming has already turned Indonesia into a major player in nickel processing, which is central to the electric vehicle supply chain. Extending that approach to other commodities could, in theory, bring more of the value, jobs and technology home.

## The Case Against: Where the Cracks Are Showing

### Fiscal strain and budget trade-offs

Big promises cost a lot. Paying for the meals program and other priorities has meant cutting spending elsewhere. Regional transfers, infrastructure maintenance and some public services have taken hits. Indonesia keeps its deficit under a legal cap of 3% of GDP, and markets watch closely for any sign that the ceiling might bend. The departure of Sri Mulyani Indrawati as finance minister in 2025 took away a figure investors had long treated as an anchor of fiscal discipline.

### Execution problems

A program as large as free meals is hard to run well. There have been repeated reports of food poisoning, uneven quality and weak oversight of supply chains. These problems make it harder to know whether the program is achieving its nutrition goals, or just spending money quickly.

### Centralization and governance risks

Danantara controls a huge pool of state assets. Critics worry about transparency, political influence and whether investment decisions will follow commercial logic or political priorities. The fund's governance may be the most important test of this presidency's economic credibility.

### Democratic backsliding concerns

Civil society groups have raised alarms on several fronts:

- A 2025 revision of the military law that widened the roles active officers can hold in civilian institutions
- A parliament with very little opposition, which weakens checks and balances
- Heavy-handed policing during protests

The unrest of August 2025 showed how quickly public frustration can boil over. It was sparked by anger over lawmakers' allowances and grew after a delivery driver was killed by a police vehicle. Those protests were about more than one incident. They reflected a sense that ordinary people carry the costs while the elite keeps the benefits.

### The growth gap

The 8% target still looks far away. Growth has hovered around 5%. Middle-class households have felt the squeeze, and job creation in the formal sector has been slow. Without stronger private investment and clearer rules, the target risks becoming a slogan instead of a plan.

## Weighing It Up

The honest assessment is that this presidency's strengths and weaknesses come from the same source: a belief in a strong, active state.

| Strength | The flip side |
|---|---|
| Fast decisions from a large coalition | Weak scrutiny and accountability |
| Ambitious welfare programs | Fiscal pressure and messy delivery |
| Consolidated state investment | Concentrated power and governance risk |
| Assertive national interest | Concerns over civil liberties and military roles |

Most of these trade-offs are not settled yet. The meals program could mature into one of the most important social investments in Indonesia's history. It could also become a warning about scaling too fast. Danantara could become a disciplined investment engine, or a vehicle for politically driven spending.

## What to Watch for the Rest of 2026

A few signals will say a lot about where things are heading:

1. **Budget discipline.** Does the deficit stay within the 3% cap without gutting regional spending?
2. **Program quality.** Is there independent evaluation of nutrition outcomes, and are food safety standards enforced?
3. **Danantara transparency.** Are there clear audits, disclosures and professional management?
4. **Space for dissent.** Are protests, journalism and civil society allowed to work freely?
5. **Investment climate.** Are there regulatory reforms that attract private capital, not just state-led projects?

## The Bottom Line

Indonesia under Prabowo is aiming high, and that deserves some credit. A country of more than 280 million people with a young population and huge natural resources should think big. But ambition only succeeds with the institutions to back it up. Budgets have to balance, programs have to deliver and power has to answer to someone. The promises are real, and so are the pitfalls. Which one wins out will depend less on the vision itself and more on the everyday work of governing well.
